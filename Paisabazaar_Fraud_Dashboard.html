The short version

The file is clean in the two ways people usually check and wrong in three ways they usually do not. No missing values, no duplicate rows, yet 5,868 rows describe someone under 18, 11,824 report a credit history longer than the person has been alive, and the score moves for 58.3% of customers inside eight months. The model lands at 68.40% against a 53.40% floor, which is useful for sorting a queue and not enough to approve a loan.

01

What is actually in the file

Three classes, badly unbalanced, and a target that will not sit still.

Standard is more than half the file. That one fact decides how every result after it has to be read, because a model that always answers "Standard" and learns nothing scores 53.2% on its own.

Read the shape, not the biggest number. Standard is nearly three times the size of Good, so any plain accuracy figure is mostly a measure of the middle group. The floor of 53.40% is what the test set gives a model that only ever guesses Standard.

Credit Mix is the cheapest signal in the file. Customers with a Bad mix are 60.1% Poor and 1.5% Good. Customers with a Good mix are 48.9% Good and 16.0% Poor. That gap comes from a field already on the application form.

Income is two columns wearing one coat. Annual_Income and Monthly_Inhand_Salary correlate at 0.998 with each other. Keeping both adds nothing and makes the model harder to explain.

Correlation and importance disagree in one place. Credit_Utilization_Ratio sits at +0.046, almost zero, yet ranks third inside the Random Forest. A tree uses a weak column in combination with others.

Outside this ladder. Num_Credit_Inquiries (−0.435) and Payment_of_Min_Amount (−0.415) both correlate strongly, but the first is a count that duplicates the loan columns and the second is nominal, so neither belongs in a numeric correlation table.

Outliers, by the IQR rule

Nearly a quarter of the file is flagged. None of it was deleted.

Why nothing was removed. Two reasons, and the second one matters more. Deleting 22.9% of the file to fix a few large values costs more than it gains. And a customer with a high income or a large outstanding loan is not a data entry error, it is a customer with a bigger loan. Those are the people a lender cares about most, so removing the rows would delete the best part of the book.

Why capping was not needed either. The model is a Random Forest, which splits on order rather than distance, so one customer earning 180,000 does not drag the fit the way it drags an average. A linear model in this notebook would have needed a cap first. That reasoning is written into Section 3 of the notebook rather than left silent.

04

The model, and what it is worth

68.40% sounds strong until the floor is 53.40%.

The 15 points are the whole result. Splitting by row instead of by customer gives 78.10%, and that extra 9.7 points is the model recognising people it already trained on. Each customer appears eight times, so a row split puts the same person on both sides.

The worst cell is not the smallest one. 1,523 of 4,405 Good customers, 34.6%, are filed as Standard. That is the model's single biggest mistake and the expensive one, because those are the customers a lender most wants to keep.

Three of these five also top the correlation ladder, so two different methods picked the same columns. Fifteen features in total, all already collected.

What it can and cannot do. 68.40% means roughly three mistakes in every ten rows. That is fine for sorting a queue and not fine for a decision about money. Used as a ranking tool with a person reviewing the borderline cases, it earns its place. Used to approve or reject automatically, it turns away 4 in 10 good customers.

05

The only real fraud signal

It lives in the two columns that were dropped before modelling.

Name and SSN are not model features, which is why they are usually dropped in the first cell. They are also the only place where an identity problem can appear, so they were kept aside and checked once.

The check needs no model and no new data. It is one line: does this name already exist against a different SSN. It can run before any money is approved, and it catches something a credit score never can, because a score describes how someone repaid and not who they are.

The honest caveat. This file looks generated, so 1,829 names with several SSNs is probably a side effect of how it was built rather than 1,829 real people. The deliverable is the check, not the names. It should flag an application for a manual look and never reject automatically, because a common name can repeat by chance.

06

What is broken, and what is not

The clean checks pass. The impossible values do not.

Age below 185,868 rows

5.87% of the file, and the youngest age is 14. A 14 year old cannot hold a credit card or a home loan, so these rows are not real. They were reported, not deleted.

Credit history longer than the person's age11,824 rows

11.82% of the file, where Credit_History_Age in months exceeds age in years × 12. Impossible on its face, and no model can repair it afterwards.

Type_of_Loan left out of the model6,261 combinations

Not an error, a modelling gap. Stored as one free-text string, so it would need splitting into separate yes and no columns first. Tested on its own it added almost nothing, because the loan count already carries the same information.

The two headline checks pass. There are zero missing values across all 28 columns and zero duplicate rows. That is why there is no imputation in this notebook, and why the cleaning work sits on impossible values instead.

What that cost the analysis. A file with no nulls looks finished, so the usual first pass finds nothing to do. The problems here only surface when a rule is applied to a value: an age below 18, a history longer than a life, a lower bound that is negative. Each of those needed a check written on purpose.

The fix belongs at the source. Correcting 17,692 impossible rows after the fact changes the data. The rules that generate the values should be corrected instead, which is why they are listed as next steps rather than patched in the notebook.

How the numbers were made

Outliers use the 1.5 × IQR rule on fifteen numeric columns. The model is a Random Forest with 100 trees on 15 features, split 75/25 with GroupShuffleSplit on Customer_ID. Every figure on this page is printed by a cell in the notebook.
